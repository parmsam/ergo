# Microbreaks (hourly 5-minute breaks)

**Used in ergo:** the "Hourly microbreak" eye rule (55 min of screen time, then a 5 min screen-off break), plus facts in the coach.
**Strength:** Trial evidence (`moderate`). Supported by NIOSH field studies and a 2022 meta-analysis.

## What the research says

- **NIOSH field study (2000).** Data-entry workers at an IRS service center compared a conventional schedule (two 15 min breaks per shift) with a supplementary schedule that added a 5 min break in each hour that didn't already have one [1]. **Discomfort in several body areas and eyestrain were significantly lower** on the supplementary schedule, **with no reduction in data-entry performance**.
- **NIOSH follow-up (2007).** With 51 workers, supplementary breaks again reliably reduced discomfort and eyestrain without hurting productivity. Adding stretching exercises showed no significant extra effect, partly because few people actually did the stretches [2].
- **Meta-analysis (2022).** Across 22 samples (N = 2,335), microbreaks of 10 minutes or less had small but significant effects on **vigor** (d = 0.36) and **fatigue** (d = 0.35) [3]. The effect on performance overall was small and not significant. Longer breaks were associated with bigger performance gains.

## How ergo applies it

- The hourly microbreak rule mirrors the NIOSH supplementary schedule: a 5 min break every hour.
- Break screens suggest eye rest (far gaze, blinking, palming), and ergo encourages getting up as well.
- You can use an hourly microbreak for eyes alongside a body rule; with "Moving rests your eyes too" on, a body break also resets the eye timer.

## Caveats

- The NIOSH studies were in intensive data-entry work. Results likely generalize to other screen-heavy work, but that's an extrapolation.
- Microbreaks reliably improve how you feel; don't expect them to boost output on demanding tasks.
